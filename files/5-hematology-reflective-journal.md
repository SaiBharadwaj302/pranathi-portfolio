# Hematology Rotation: Reflective Journal

**Cambridge Health Alliance (CHA), a Harvard Medical School affiliate, Cambridge, MA** | April 2026

**Instruments:** Sysmex XN-3100, CellaVision, ACL TOP 350, BD FACSLyric

---

## Overview

My hematology rotation at CHA was a really valuable experience because it gave me exposure to multiple areas within the lab, not just one section. I worked on the specimen processing and loading bench, where I learned how important it is to first check the integrity of the sample before doing anything else. From there, I worked with the Sysmex hematology analyzer, which performs CBC testing and prepares slides that are later analyzed using CellaVision. I also had the opportunity to observe some flow cytometry for T lymphocyte subsets, which added another layer to my understanding of hematology.

## Scope Bench

One of the most important parts of my experience was working at the scope bench. Here, I used CellaVision to analyze abnormal blood samples, reconfirm results, perform platelet estimates, and group white blood cells appropriately. It also involved deciding whether a slide needed further review by the pathologist. This really helped me understand the responsibility involved in hematology, because even small observations can impact diagnosis.

## Coagulation Bench

I also spent time at the coagulation bench, where I worked with the ACL TOP analyzer. I learned how to prepare proper dilutions for reagents and run quality control. One thing that surprised me here was how sensitive the tests are to temperature and volume. Even small variations can affect results, which made me realize how careful and precise we need to be in this area.

## Connecting Theory to Practice

My theoretical knowledge definitely helped me throughout this rotation, especially when I was at the scope bench. For example, when I saw left shifted white blood cells, I was able to connect them to possible underlying conditions like infections or leukemias. This made the experience more meaningful because I could actually understand what I was seeing instead of just identifying cells.

One thing that stood out to me was that not all cells look exactly like textbook images. Some cells can appear slightly different, which can be confusing at first. However, reviewing them again and discussing them with the trainer or pathologist helped me understand how to interpret these variations. It also showed me how these findings are eventually correlated with bone marrow smears and the overall clinical picture to reach a diagnosis.

## Cases Observed

During my rotation, I saw a variety of cases, including anemia, abnormal white blood cell counts with left shift, and both increased and decreased platelet counts. I also had the opportunity to observe blasts and target cells, which was really interesting and helped reinforce my learning. I was able to see both automated and manual methods, which gave me a better understanding of how they complement each other.

The automated work on the Sysmex was easier for me to handle, but what required more thinking was comparing current results with previous patient results and correlating them with the patient's diagnosis. This helped me understand whether results were valid or needed to be repeated.

## Reflection

After this experience, I feel much more confident in reading CBC results and interpreting blood smears. I also feel more comfortable trusting my observations while still being careful and detail oriented. This rotation made me realize how important hematology is in understanding a patient's condition, whether it is before or after surgery, or during treatment.

Overall, this experience will be very helpful for my future as a medical laboratory professional. It taught me to pay attention to details, trust my knowledge, and always look at the full picture, including previous results, clinical symptoms, and lab findings, before making decisions. It also reinforced the importance of asking for a second opinion when needed and continuously learning from each case I encounter.
